# [DOA Estimation] Sound Source Signal and Direction of Arrival Estimation Based on Maximal Likelihood Estimation Combined with Gaussian Mixture Models and Feature Analysis [with MATLAB Code]

I. Summary This paper proposes an estimation method for the source signal and its arrival direction (DOA). The method is based on the maximum likelihood (ML) estimation of the transfer function between microphones, combined with the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm of the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM). It assumes that each microphone captures signals at a certain delay due to sound propagation and accompanied by certain attenuation. Through this modeling approach, finding the maximum log likelihood value in the ML estimation process can be easily achieved through simple feature value decomposition of a properly designed matrix.
Computer simulation results show that regardless of the amplitude differences between the signals between microphones, the proposed method's Signal Distortion Ratio (SDR) can exceed 10dB, and the average error in DOA estimation is less than 8 degrees. Moreover, this method maintains high performance under various conditions.
II. Introduction In audio signal processing, extracting target source signals from interference speech, environmental noise, and other competing signals is a crucial task. Estimating the arrival direction of the source signal is also significant for event detection at different scales, such as everyday scenarios to volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, tsunamis, and so on. Therefore, several multi-microphone-based algorithms have been proposed for these tasks [1,2].
Previous research often assumes that the microphone placement distance is sufficient to ensure that the signal amplitudes captured by each microphone are at the same level, and only the arrival time difference exists. Under this assumption, Izumi et al. emphasized the sparsity of speech in both time and frequency domains, proposing a blind source separation (BSS) method based on maximum likelihood estimation combined with time-frequency masking in reverberant environments [3]. However, this method has a significant drawback: when estimating the arrival time difference between microphones, it must use a brute force search method.
The Maruyama group has improved the aforementioned method by introducing analytical updates for time-delay estimation [4]. This approach models the delay not only as a function of the source direction but also as a function of frequency. By taking the derivative of the auxiliary function, analytical updates for the delay parameters are derived theoretically, resulting in a function shape similar to the Frobenius distribution [5]. Although this method significantly reduces computation time by avoiding brute force search, it still requires an iterative process of the EM algorithm and assumes a fixed difference in signal amplitude between microphones.
Inspired by previous research [4], this study adopts a different approach: it assumes that not only is there a delay when each microphone captures the signal, but there is also signal attenuation. This assumption is reasonable because the application scenarios considered include not only speech processing but also subsonic monitoring. In subsonic monitoring, signals have very low frequencies, necessitating microphones placed at a greater distance to achieve direction of arrival estimation, which inevitably leads to signal attenuation during propagation [6]. By this new modeling approach, without the need for the EM algorithm, maximum likelihood values can be directly maximized through feature value decomposition.

IV. Conclusion\tThis paper assumes that simultaneous time differences and amplitude differences exist among signals captured by multiple microphones, proposing a new method for simultaneously estimating the direction of arrival (DOA) and source signals. This method is based on the maximum likelihood estimation of the time difference for each time-frequency component, combined with the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm of the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), with the core advantage being that the log likelihood value is maximized directly through feature value decomposition without the iterative process required by traditional methods, while also being compatible with multi-scene requirements such as speech processing and subsonic monitoring.
Computer simulation results further verify the effectiveness of the method: regardless of whether the signal amplitude differences between microphones are significant, the proposed method's Signal Distortion Ratio (SDR) consistently exceeds 10dB, meeting the engineering standards for high-quality signal extraction. The average estimation error in direction of arrival is controlled within 8 degrees, maintaining high positioning accuracy even in scenarios of medium to long distance monitoring. Moreover, even under complex conditions with noise intensity changes and dynamic adjustments of the number of sources, the method can maintain performance stability by constraining the frequency range from fL to fH and modeling the noise as a Gaussian distribution.
However, there are areas for optimization in this study: firstly, the direct use of the Gaussian distribution obtained by the EM algorithm as the modeling approach for source signal probability, which has shown effective results in experiments but lacks rigorous theoretical derivation support, necessitating further validation of its applicability in extreme noise environments; secondly, the current method has a strong dependency on predefined source numbers, and deviations from these preset values in actual scenarios may lead to decreased clustering accuracy. Future research could explore improved solutions that incorporate adaptive detection of source numbers.
Overall, this method expands the application boundaries of multi-microphone signal processing by breaking away from the traditional assumption of "undifferentiated amplitudes between microphones," particularly valuable in applications such as sub-audible monitoring and long-distance speech enhancement, providing theoretical references and experimental evidence for the subsequent engineering implementation of related technologies.
